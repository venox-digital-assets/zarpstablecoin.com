**Dollar stablecoins won the first wave. The next will put real assets on-chain, and can't run on dollars alone.**

Nearly 99% of stablecoins are denominated in US dollars, according to the IMF. Roughly two-thirds of the supply, by an estimate cited by Goldman Sachs, is held by individuals in emerging markets. These are astonishing numbers and, read together, describe the first wave of stablecoin adoption. People in places with unstable currencies and patchy banking found a way to hold dollars without a dollar bank account. That wave is real, but it's no longer the interesting part of the story. The next one is already under way, and it's about assets rather than savings.

Treasury bills, money market funds, bonds, private credit and property are tokenising and moving onto blockchains. Every one of those assets is denominated in a currency. A tokenised rand bond has to be paid for in rand. If there's no rand on the same network, it can't settle there. Put simply: any asset brought on-chain needs corresponding liquidity in local currency.

That is the case for local-currency, or non-dollar stablecoins. The world will keep issuing assets in many currencies. If those assets are going to trade on-chain, each of those currencies needs liquid, trustworthy money on-chain too.

Stablecoin supply grew more than tenfold between the end of 2020 and the end of 2025, to around $300 billion. Most of that growth came from places where the dollar solved a problem. In Venezuela, TRM Labs found that stablecoin use is driven by domestic economic and political pressures rather than speculative cycles. Remittances are another driver: eight of the top ten remittance destinations are emerging markets.

South Africa followed the same pattern on a smaller scale. The Reserve Bank reports that dollar-stablecoin trading on local platforms grew from under R4 billion in 2022 to almost R80 billion in the year to October 2025, and that dollar stablecoins have replaced Bitcoin as the preferred trading pair.

We should be careful with the big numbers. The IMF notes that much stablecoin activity still happens inside the crypto ecosystem, driven by bots and algorithmic arbitrage, and puts payment-related flows at about $390 billion in 2025; a small slice of the headline volume. The first wave proved there is demand for digital money. It didn't prove that dollar tokens are the right money for everything.

Tokenised real-world assets, excluding stablecoins, grew from about $8 billion at the start of 2024 to roughly $38 billion by August 2026, according to rwa.xyz. Tokenised US Treasuries and money market funds from firms such as BlackRock and Franklin Templeton make up a large share.

Every one of those trades has two legs: the asset, and the cash that pays for it. On a blockchain, both legs can settle in the same instant, so that either both move or neither does. The European Central Bank put it plainly in May 2026: stablecoins have become the default cash leg for this kind of atomic settlement, and dollar stablecoins, through first-mover advantage and network effects, have become the default settlement asset in tokenised finance.

That isn't an accident. Almost everything tokenised so far is a dollar asset, because dollars are the only cash with deep liquidity on-chain. The assets followed the money.

## Why dollars can't settle everything

Picture a South African asset manager buying a tokenised rand bond. If the only on-chain cash is dollars, the trade needs two currency conversions, and someone carries exchange-rate risk in between. It also routes a domestic transaction through a foreign currency, which raises exchange-control questions for buyer and seller alike. Institutions with rand liabilities, such as pension funds and insurers, face limits on offshore exposure and have little reason to take that detour.

The alternative is to settle off-chain through the banking system, which gives up much of the point of tokenising the asset in the first place. This is the core issue with many existing "blockchain" solutions that claim to tokenise a real world asset while really only offering the worst of both (traditional finance and crypto) worlds. Either way, a rand asset without on-chain rand is a half-finished product.

The same logic applies to every currency in which real assets are issued: the Euro, the Yen, the Real, the Naira, the Rand. Europe has already reached that conclusion. The ECB is connecting its wholesale settlement system to DLT platforms, and Euro stablecoin volumes rose twelvefold in 15 months to about $777 million a month, according to TRM Labs. The debate in Europe is no longer whether Euro-denominated tokenised markets need on-chain euros, but how to provide them safely.

The Dollar won the first wave because people wanted dollars. In the second wave, the asset decides the currency, and most of the world's assets aren't in dollars.

## Why demand looks low, and why that's the wrong measure

At ZARP we issue a Rand stablecoin, so we should be honest about the evidence. In a speech at the University of Cape Town in August 2026, IMF First Deputy Managing Director Dan Katz noted that Rand-linked stablecoins in South Africa have seen even less demand than Dollar ones, though he said it was too early to draw firm conclusions.

That is the right observation measured against the wrong job. As a place to park savings, a Rand token will lose to a Dollar token; that is the Dollar's home ground. Settlement is different. Demand for on-chain rands follows Rand assets on-chain, and there are very few of those yet. It's a chicken-and-egg problem, and the liquidity has to come first: no issuer will tokenise a Rand bond if there's nothing to settle it in.

There are nearer-term uses too. A company paying dozens of suppliers in rands still relies on batched EFTs that settle on business days, and instant options like PayShap carry per-bank transaction limits that make them awkward for bulk payment runs. A rand stablecoin settles in seconds, at any hour, with a record that can be reconciled automatically, and nobody involved has to take a view on the Dollar.

## The objections we take seriously

The IMF raises a fair one. When a local-currency stablecoin and a Dollar stablecoin sit on the same blockchain, users can swap between them on-chain, outside banks and regulated currency dealers. That could make Dollar stablecoins easier to reach, not harder.

Central banks raise another. The ECB worries that settling tokenised markets in competing private tokens fragments money, and wants central bank money as the anchor. Banks argue that tokenised deposits make a better cash leg for institutions. The Reserve Bank's own Project Khokha 2 tested both a wholesale central bank token and a bank-issued settlement token for tokenised debentures.

We don't think these options compete. Wholesale central bank money can anchor the system, and tokenised deposits will suit banks settling with their own clients. But open, public networks are where global investors and much of tokenised finance already operate, and they need a bearer Rand token that anyone can hold. Our answer to the swap concern is to tie a Rand stablecoin into regulated on-ramps, off-ramps and reporting, which is where the IMF itself says oversight should focus. That keeps the points where rand enters and leaves the system visible, which is appropriate and doesn't hinder on-chain activity that makes crypto desirable in the first place.

## Where ZARP stands

As at 18 October 2026, there were 81.9 million ZARP in circulation, with Ethereum holding about half and the rest spread across Base, Solana and Polygon. Reserves of R93.4 million, held in cash and money-market instruments and managed by Old Mutual Wealth, cover that supply 1.14 times over. The token is being used: R68 million in ZARP moved on-chain in the past 28 days, equivalent to more than 80% of the supply, and more than 390,000 on-chain transactions have been recorded since launch.

Those numbers are small next to the Dollar giants, and we'd rather say so than dress them up. But they show that a fully reserved, independently attested rand token already works on public chains, and that is the piece of infrastructure rand tokenisation will need. Also, it's early days.

## What has to happen next

Four things would turn this from a niche into infrastructure.

1. **Clear rules.** The Reserve Bank has compiled data on crypto custody and is drafting a Crypto Asset Manual. A stablecoin framework would give banks and asset managers the certainty to commit.
2. **Rand assets on-chain.** Issuers, asset managers and exchanges need to pilot tokenised rand instruments, starting with the simplest: money market funds, short-dated debt and invoices.
3. **Dependable routes in and out of the banking system.** A stablecoin must have bridges to the currency that backs it, and that bridge is still the weakest part.
4. **Interoperability with the Reserve Bank's wholesale work,** so that private rand tokens and central bank money can settle against each other.

## Where to start

If you issue, manage or settle Rand assets, the first step is modest. Pick one flow where settlement is slow, manual or expensive, such as supplier payments, fund subscriptions or collateral movements, and run a pilot you can measure.

The first wave of stablecoins made the Dollar available to anyone with a phone. The second will put the world's assets on-chain, and those assets will settle in the currencies they're issued in. Each of those currencies will need its own on-chain cash. For the Rand, we're providing it with ZARP.
